## Decision

Stop #953's proposed high-limb radix specialization as a compiler-throughput
change on the measured routes. The large 3,564,726-token compiler unity input,
a small aggregate compile, an `__int128` fixture, and a verbose unity compile
called the common integer formatter, but **none** supplied a nonzero high limb
or selected binary, octal or hexadecimal formatting. The 128-iteration division
path therefore executed zero times on those workloads. This is a negative
population result, not an assertion that the formatter is globally unused or
that a standalone `u128` formatter cannot be made faster.

The instrumented GCC `-O0` unity compile recorded 1,145,100 ns inside all
radix conversions, including per-call clock reads, against 9,641,773,585 ns
enclosing subprocess time (0.0119%). This one noisy diagnostic sample is a
screen, not a trusted Release/Zen 5 speedup or a transferable upper bound.
There is no optimized scalar or SIMD implementation, no A/B result and no
compiler-wide improvement claim. Reopen the candidate only with an identified
compiler route that actually formats wide values often enough to matter.

## Scope of the conclusion

The common formatter still handled 14,724 low-width decimal calls during the
nonverbose unity compile. Its low-half hardware-division branch is distinct
from the repeated 128-step high-half loop motivating #953. An optimization to
common low-width decimal printing would need a separate attribution and
matched-build experiment; this audit does not propose one. Formatting-heavy
commands or error paths may have different populations and are not ruled out
by this sample. The negative result is retained so a future investigation can
start from its exact source, routes and probe rather than assuming wide
formatting is a current compiler bottleneck.
